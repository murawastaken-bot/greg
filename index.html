Gibbons v. Ogden
and Heffernan v. City of Paterson

A quick rundown of two Supreme Court cases

Gibbons v. Ogden1824

What happened?

  • New York gave Robert Livingston and Robert Fulton a monopoly. They were the only ones allowed to run steamboats in New York waters.
  • Aaron Ogden had a license from them to run a steamboat between New York and New Jersey.
  • Thomas Gibbons ran a steamboat on the same route. He had a federal license from Congress, not a New York one.
  • Ogden sued Gibbons to make him stop.

The big question

The Constitution says Congress can regulate "commerce among the several states." Does commerce just mean buying and selling things, or does it also mean traveling and shipping across state lines?

What the Court decided

Chief Justice John Marshall said commerce includes transportation, like steamboats. Congress controls trade between states, and federal law beats state law, so New York's monopoly could not stop Gibbons. Gibbons won.

Why it matters today

This is why Congress can make rules for things like airplanes, trains, and the internet that cross state lines.

Heffernan v. City of Paterson2016

What happened?

  • Jeffrey Heffernan was a police officer in Paterson, New Jersey.
  • His mom was sick and asked him to pick up a campaign sign for a person running against the mayor.
  • The police chief saw him with the sign and thought he supported the other candidate.
  • The chief demoted him. But Heffernan was not even helping the campaign. He was just getting the sign for his mom!

The big question

The First Amendment protects free speech and political choices. Heffernan did not actually do anything political. Can the government still be in trouble for punishing him for something it only thought he did?

What the Court decided

The Court voted 6 to 2 that yes, it is still unconstitutional. What matters is the reason the government punished him. If a boss punishes a worker to discourage politics, it is wrong even if the boss guessed wrong. Heffernan won. Justice Breyer wrote the opinion.

Why it matters today

Government workers can be involved in politics without being scared of getting punished because of a rumor or mistake.

Why loose construction is better

Loose construction means reading the Constitution by its purpose, not just its exact words. Strict construction means sticking only to the exact words.

Reason 1: It works with new technology (Gibbons)

The Founders had never seen a steamboat. If "commerce" only meant trading goods in 1787, then Congress could not deal with new things like boats, planes, or the internet. Marshall read it broadly so the words still worked.

Reason 2: It protects what rights are really for (Heffernan)

A strict reading could say, "Heffernan didn't really speak or join a campaign, so the First Amendment doesn't apply." But the point of the First Amendment is to stop the government from punishing people over politics. The Court looked at that purpose, so workers are protected.

Reason 3: The Constitution was made to last

The Founders used big, broad words on purpose. Loose construction lets the Constitution keep working without rewriting it every time something changes.

The other side: People who like strict construction say judges might use "purpose" to push their own opinions, and that the people should change the Constitution with amendments instead. That is a fair point, but in both of these cases, reading it more broadly gave answers that matched what the Constitution was made to do.

Vocab

  • Commerce: business and trade, including transportation
  • Interstate: between two or more states
  • Monopoly: when only one person or company is allowed to sell something
  • Supremacy Clause: federal law is the "supreme law of the land" and beats state law
  • First Amendment: protects speech, religion, press, assembly, and petition
  • Demote: to move someone to a lower job
Sources: Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. 1 (1824); Heffernan v. City of Paterson, 578 U.S. 266 (2016)
